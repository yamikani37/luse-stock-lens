ZAFFICO: the rights issue, explained

ZAFFICO grows pine and eucalyptus plantations and is moving into wood manufacturing. In July it offered every shareholder one new share for each share held, at a deep discount, to fund that plan.

Copperbelt Energy: a dollar business priced in kwacha

CEC supplies power, mostly to copper mines in Zambia and the DRC. It reports in US dollars, but its shares trade in kwacha, so a stronger kwacha shrinks the kwacha value of the same dollar profits.

For a client: CEC is partly a currency position. If the kwacha keeps strengthening, the share can fall even while the business grows. If it weakens, the reverse.

Econometric analysis: what is driving each price

Every figure in this section is recalculated from the price data each time the page loads, using the same steps for both companies. Each price move is split into known effects, the market and a company-specific remainder, and the remainder is then tested against the stock's normal day-to-day noise. Each finding is labelled with how much weight it can bear.

      Method

      1. Split each move into its parts

      Log price changes add up, so a move can be split exactly. For ZAFFICO, from the last close before the rights issue: the dilution from issuing one share at K2.53 per old share (log of expected price after ÷ price before), the market, and a remainder. For Copperbelt Energy, from 1 January: the change in kwacha per dollar (CEC reports in dollars, so a stronger kwacha lowers its kwacha value one-for-one), the market, and a remainder.

      2. The market's share

      With at least 20 matched observations, the market's share uses a Dimson (1979) beta: the stock's returns are regressed on the LASI's returns over the same period, the period before and the period after, and the three betas are summed. This corrects the bias thin trading puts on an ordinary beta. Observations are weighted by the number of trading days they cover. With less data, beta is set to 1 (the market-adjusted model, Brown and Warner 1985).

      3. Is the remainder unusual?

      The company-specific remainder is divided by the stock's volatility scaled to the length of the window (z = remainder ÷ (daily volatility × √trading days)), and compared with a t distribution. Daily volatility is estimated from irregularly spaced prices, assuming variance grows with trading days. A big remainder that isn't significant means the stock is too volatile, or the data too thin, to call it more than noise.

      4. ZAFFICO: pull back towards the expected price

      A discrete Ornstein–Uhlenbeck regression on the log gap between the price and the theoretical ex-rights price, using prices since the ex-date: change in gap = −κ × trading days × previous gap. A significant κ > 0 means gaps close over time, with half-life ln 2 ÷ κ. The expected price assumes full take-up of the new shares.

      5. Copperbelt Energy: is it priced in dollars?

      Once 20 matched days are collected, CEC's daily returns are regressed on the change in kwacha per dollar and on the LASI. An elasticity of 1 means the market prices CEC as a dollar asset; 0 means no currency link. Both are tested.

      Limits

      Before daily collection builds up, history comes from about 20 hand-collected closing prices per stock, so most tests have little power. LuSE holidays are treated as trading days. Thin trading makes prices stale, which understates volatility for stocks like CEC. Collected days where price and volume are both unchanged from the day before are treated as no fresh data.
